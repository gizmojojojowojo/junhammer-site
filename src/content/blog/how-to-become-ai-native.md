**Key takeaways**

- Becoming AI-native is mostly operational work. Birdview's CEO, with three AI agents in production, found that most of the hours went into [preparing the rest of the company so the agents could do their jobs at all](https://birdviewpsa.com/?p=81041).
- Start with rules. Madewithlove advises writing down [how people are allowed to use the tools already in front of them](https://madewithlove.com/blog/how-to-become-an-ai-native-saas-company-in-the-next-3-years/) before thinking about agents.
- Write the company down. McKinsey's AI-native companies found that [accessing tribal knowledge](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/business-building/our-insights/the-seven-operating-truths-of-AI-native-companies), not the AI model, is the bottleneck.
- Rebuild one workflow at a time. AWS's advice to small and mid-size businesses: [pilot quickly, measure results, and expand in small waves](https://aws.amazon.com/smart-business/resources-for-smb/ai-readiness-checklist/).
- Test AI output against known correct answers before trusting it, and give AI more independence only as it earns it.
- Expect roles to change. Bain says AI leaves [judgment, not capacity, as the scarce resource](https://www.bain.com/insights/an-operating-model-for-the-age-of-ai/).

You cannot go back and found your company around AI. You can rebuild how it runs. This roadmap is for an established business, the 20, 50 or 500 person company that runs on people, email and spreadsheets, that wants AI doing real work rather than sitting in a few browser tabs. If you are not sure what "AI-native" means for a company like yours, start with [What is an AI-native company?](/blog/what-is-an-ai-native-company/)

## How does an established company become AI-native?

Set rules for AI use, write down how the company works, pick one high-volume workflow, and rebuild it so AI does the routine steps. Test it, measure it, then repeat with the next workflow. The company changes one process at a time.

The steps below follow that order. BCG recommends the same broad sequence to traditional firms: [first building the foundation, then proving it in one place, and finally scaling it](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/companies-use-ai-to-redesign-work).

## Step 1: Set the rules and approved tools

Decide which AI tools staff may use, what data can go into them, and when a person must review AI output. Give people company-managed accounts so work stops flowing through personal ones.

Madewithlove's advice is to do this before anything ambitious. Someone in your company is already using AI, and without an approved tool [they may be using a personal account](https://madewithlove.com/blog/how-to-become-an-ai-native-saas-company-in-the-next-3-years/) and deciding for themselves what company data is safe to paste in.

A one or two page policy is enough to start. My post on [AI integration services](/blog/ai-integration-services/) covers what to put in it.

## Step 2: Write the company down

Document how your key processes actually run, where the data lives, who owns each system, and the informal rules people follow. AI can only do work it can understand and reach.

This is the step most companies underestimate. Birdview's CEO describes it as writing down the decisions people have been making informally, such as [which system is the source of truth and what counts as an active customer](https://birdviewpsa.com/?p=81041). In his words, becoming AI-native is a cross-functional operating change.

McKinsey heard the same from AI-native companies: the teams pulling ahead invest in what their AI can find, because [your model isn't the bottleneck; accessing tribal knowledge is](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/business-building/our-insights/the-seven-operating-truths-of-AI-native-companies).

Start with the processes you plan to rebuild first. You do not need to document the whole company before you begin.

## Step 3: Check your readiness

Before building, confirm you have what the first workflow needs: reachable data with a clear owner, tools that can connect, rules for privacy and review, and a way to measure results.

AWS's readiness checklist for small and mid-size businesses covers the essentials. It advises you to [confirm your data sources, owners, and access rules before you automate](https://aws.amazon.com/smart-business/resources-for-smb/ai-readiness-checklist/) and to [start with low-effort tools that integrate with systems you already use](https://aws.amazon.com/smart-business/resources-for-smb/ai-readiness-checklist/).

A good readiness check also tells you what not to build. Some workflows are not worth automating yet.

## Step 4: Pick one workflow to rebuild

Choose a workflow that happens often, follows clear rules, uses data you can reach, and has a result you can measure. Rebuild it so AI does the routine steps and people handle exceptions and decisions.

Score candidates on four questions:

| Question | Good sign | Warning sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How often does it happen? | Daily or weekly, many times | A few times a year |
| Are the rules clear? | Staff could write them down | It depends on who you ask |
| Can AI reach the data? | It lives in systems with access controls | It lives in people's heads or inboxes |
| Can you measure the result? | Time, accuracy or speed you can count | "It feels better" |

Typical first workflows for a services business:

- **Lead intake:** read each inquiry, sort it by type and urgency, file it in the CRM with a drafted reply, and create a task for the right person.
- **Recurring reports:** pull the data, summarize changes, format the report for review.
- **Internal questions:** answer staff or customer questions from your own documents, with links to the source.

Madewithlove puts it simply: [start with one bounded experiment in a workflow where people already struggle](https://madewithlove.com/blog/how-to-become-an-ai-native-saas-company-in-the-next-3-years/).

## Step 5: Build it, test it, then ship it

Build the workflow, then test it against a set of real or realistic cases with known correct answers before it goes live. Run it alongside the current process first so you can compare.

Write the test set before you judge the results. Include the hard cases on purpose: spam, incomplete information, other languages, questions the AI should decline. When I built [a lead intake demo for a roofing company](/work/roofing-lead-intake/), the test set included spam, a Spanish voicemail, repeat callers and leads missing a phone number. It sorted every test message into the right category, and the first live run exposed setup problems that the test set alone would not have caught.

For agents that make more decisions, test more than once. My [open-source map agent](/work/map-agent/) is checked against core questions, deliberately hard questions and a fresh set written after the last fix, with each run repeated.

Keep a person in the loop where mistakes are expensive. McKinsey's companies follow the rule that [trust precedes autonomy](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/business-building/our-insights/the-seven-operating-truths-of-AI-native-companies): AI earns more independence over time, and human approval stays wherever an error would be costly.

## Step 6: Measure, then expand in waves

Track a few numbers for each workflow, such as time per task, error rate and how often people override the AI. When a workflow holds up, move to the next one, and widen access in stages.

AWS recommends you [define one business outcome for your first AI pilot and how you'll measure it](https://aws.amazon.com/smart-business/resources-for-smb/ai-readiness-checklist/). Bain warns against the opposite habit: measuring progress by [counting pilots](https://www.bain.com/insights/what-is-an-ai-native-enterprise/) rather than by what redesigned workflows actually changed.

The documentation, data access and testing habits you build for one workflow carry over to the next.

## What changes in roles and the org chart?

People move from doing routine work to directing and checking AI, and decisions shift toward whoever owns the outcome. Leaders spend more time setting direction and less coordinating tasks.

Bain's [An Operating Model for the Age of AI](https://www.bain.com/insights/an-operating-model-for-the-age-of-ai/) (May 2026) describes three shifts: from hierarchy to orchestrated outcomes, from tasks to judgment and orchestration, and from coordination to scaled judgment. It predicts org charts will become accountability charts.

BCG saw the same pattern in practice. In its front-runner firms, work shifts [from people producing output to people directing and evaluating AI](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/companies-use-ai-to-redesign-work), and new roles appear, such as workflow designers and agent shepherds.

For a smaller company, the practical version is simple. Give each AI workflow a business owner who is accountable for its results. McKinsey's companies [centralize the platform and decentralize the tasks](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/business-building/our-insights/the-seven-operating-truths-of-AI-native-companies): a small central role handles tools, security and data access, while each team decides how to use AI on its own problems.

## Why do AI projects stall?

Most stall for operational reasons, not technical ones: no owner, no access to the data, no test set and no measurement. Spreading effort across many small pilots makes all four worse.

Bain found that most companies pursuing AI transformation are managing [a portfolio of pilots and incremental tools](https://www.bain.com/insights/what-is-an-ai-native-enterprise/) with investment spread across dozens of initiatives. Birdview's CEO adds that when an agent produces odd results, [the reason is almost never the model](https://birdviewpsa.com/?p=81041). It is usually missing context the company never wrote down.

## How long does it take?

There is no single finish line. Each workflow is its own project, and the pace depends mostly on how well your processes and data are documented. A company that has written down how it works can move from one workflow to the next much faster.

## Frequently asked questions

**How does an established company become AI-native?**
Set rules for AI use, write down how the company works, pick one high-volume workflow, rebuild it so AI does the routine steps, test it against known answers, measure the results, and repeat with the next workflow.

**Do we need a data team before we start?**
No. Most first workflows run on tools you already have. What you need is someone who knows the process, access to the data the workflow uses, and a builder who can connect the pieces and test them.

**What should we automate first?**
Pick a workflow that happens often, follows clear rules, uses data you can reach, and has a result you can measure. Lead intake, recurring reports and answering common questions from your own documents are common starting points.

**Do we need custom software?**
Usually not for the first workflows. Many run on your existing CRM, project tools and an AI model connected through integrations. Custom software makes sense when off-the-shelf tools cannot reach your data or meet your accuracy needs.

**Who should own AI inside the company?**
Give each workflow a business owner who is accountable for its results, and keep a small central role for tools, security and data access. McKinsey's AI-native companies centralize the platform and decentralize the tasks.

**How long does it take to become AI-native?**
There is no single finish line. Each workflow is its own project, and the pace depends mostly on how well your processes and data are documented. Companies that have written down how they work move faster.
