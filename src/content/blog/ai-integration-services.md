## Key takeaways

- **AI integration services connect AI to the way your business already works.** That means your workflows, data, approval steps, and rules, so the time savings show up in the business and not only in one person's browser tab.
- **Your team probably uses AI already, even if your company does not.** In Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 survey, [75% of knowledge workers used AI at work and 78% of those users brought their own tools](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2024/05/08/microsoft-and-linkedin-release-the-2024-work-trend-index-on-the-state-of-ai-at-work/).
- **Unmanaged AI costs real money.** IBM found that organizations with high levels of shadow AI paid [an average of $670,000 more per data breach](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2025-07-30-ibm-report-13-of-organizations-reported-breaches-of-ai-models-or-applications,-97-of-which-reported-lacking-proper-ai-access-controls).
- **Using AI is common. Getting business results from it is not.** In McKinsey's 2026 survey, [80% of respondents said AI improved their own productivity, but only 37% attributed any EBIT impact to it](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai).
- **The path from zero AI to AI everywhere has six steps:** a readiness assessment, a usage policy, one measured pilot, the right tools, team training, and a department-by-department rollout.
- **Outside help raises the odds of deployment.** MIT NANDA's research found [buying from specialized vendors and building with partners succeeded about 67% of the time, while internal builds succeeded far less often](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/).

Your company may have no official AI tools, no AI policy, and no AI budget. Your employees are almost certainly using AI anyway. AI integration services close that gap. They take a business from scattered, unofficial experiments to AI built into the workflows that run the company, with rules, measurement, and training in place.

This guide walks through that path one stage at a time. It covers how to assess where you stand, which workflows to start with, how to roll AI out across departments, and when it makes sense to bring in an [AI consultant](/services/ai-consulting/).

## What are AI integration services?

AI integration services connect AI tools to a company's existing workflows, software, and data so the technology does useful work inside the business. A typical engagement covers four things: finding the tasks worth handing to AI, choosing and configuring the tools, setting rules for safe use, and training the people who will use it every day.

AI integration consulting is the planning half of that work. An AI integration consultant studies how your team works today and decides what to change. The implementation half is the build: connecting tools, writing prompts and review steps, and, where off-the-shelf software falls short, building [custom AI tools](/services/ai-engineering/).

The difference from simply buying software is ownership of the outcome. A license gives people access to a chatbot. Integration changes how a report gets built, how a lead gets qualified, or how a support ticket gets answered, and it tracks whether that change saved time.

## Your team already uses AI. Just not officially.

A company with no AI policy usually still has employees using AI every day. In the [2024 Work Trend Index](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2024/05/08/microsoft-and-linkedin-release-the-2024-work-trend-index-on-the-state-of-ai-at-work/), a survey of 31,000 workers in 31 countries, 75% of knowledge workers said they used AI at work. Of those users, 78% brought their own AI tools rather than using tools their employer provided. Only 39% had received AI training from their company.

This unsanctioned use is called shadow AI. It usually starts with good intentions: someone pastes a messy email thread into a chatbot to get a summary, or drafts a proposal with a free tool. The work gets done faster. The company just has no idea what data went where, which outputs were checked, or which shortcuts are worth copying across the team.

## Is unofficial AI use a risk for my business?

Yes. Unmanaged AI use raises both security risk and breach costs. IBM's [2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2025-07-30-ibm-report-13-of-organizations-reported-breaches-of-ai-models-or-applications,-97-of-which-reported-lacking-proper-ai-access-controls) found:

- One in five organizations reported a breach due to shadow AI.
- Only 37% had policies to manage AI or detect shadow AI.
- Organizations with high levels of shadow AI paid an average of $670,000 more per breach than those with little or none.
- Of the organizations that reported breaches of AI models or applications, 97% lacked proper AI access controls.

Banning AI rarely fixes this, because people keep using tools that save them time. The safer move is to adopt AI on purpose: approve the tools, set clear rules for what data can go into them, and give people a better option than the free tool they found on their own.

## The 5 stages of AI adoption

A good AI adoption strategy starts by naming where you are today. Most businesses move through five stages. Each stage has a clear signal and a clear next move.

| Stage | What it looks like | Main risk | Next move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Unofficial | No approved tools. Individuals use free AI tools on their own. | Company data in unapproved tools, with no visibility | Find out what people already use |
| 2. Sanctioned | Approved tools and a written usage policy exist. | Licenses bought, habits unchanged | Pick one workflow to redesign |
| 3. Assisted | AI drafts, summarizes, and researches inside a few workflows, with human review. | Gains stay with a few enthusiasts | Document the workflow and train the team |
| 4. Automated | AI runs multi-step workflows across systems, such as intake, reporting, or QA. | Errors repeat at scale if checks are weak | Add review steps and measure accuracy |
| 5. AI-native | Every department has AI in its core workflows, with owners and metrics. | Tool sprawl and rising costs | Review costs and results each quarter |

Most businesses reading this are in stage 1 or 2. That is a normal place to start. The data shows how much room there is: as of May 2026, the U.S. Census Bureau found that [19.8% of U.S. firms used AI in their business](https://census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html), with use far more common at large firms than small ones.

## What is an AI readiness assessment?

An AI readiness assessment is a structured review of your workflows, tools, data, people, and risks that shows where AI will pay off and what has to be in place first. It ends with a ranked list of opportunities, so you start with the work that saves the most time at the lowest risk.

A useful assessment covers five areas:

1. **Current AI use.** Which tools people already use, for what tasks, and with what data.
2. **Workflows.** Where people copy and paste between systems, rebuild the same report every month, or answer the same questions repeatedly.
3. **Data.** Where your information lives, who can access it, and what must never leave your systems.
4. **People.** Who is already skilled with AI, who is skeptical, and who will own each workflow.
5. **Risk and rules.** Regulatory limits, client contracts, and review steps your industry requires.

### A quick self-check

Answer yes or no to each question. Every "no" is a gap your readiness assessment should close.

- Do you know which AI tools your employees use today?
- Do you have a written policy on what data can go into AI tools?
- Can you name three tasks your team repeats every week that follow the same steps?
- Does someone own the decision about which AI tools to approve?
- Do you have a way to measure time saved or quality gained?

In my [AI consulting](/services/ai-consulting/) engagements, this assessment is the discovery phase: interviews with the people doing the work, a review of your tools and data, and an opportunity map that ranks each AI use case by effort, risk, and payoff.

## Your AI implementation roadmap: from zero to every department

An AI implementation roadmap turns the readiness assessment into a sequence of steps. Each step builds the foundation for the next one. Skipping ahead is how companies end up with expensive licenses and unchanged habits.

### Step 1: Run the AI readiness assessment

Start with the review described above. The output is a short, ranked list of AI use cases and a clear picture of the data and rules you are working with.

**How I help:** I interview the people doing the work and map where AI saves real time. You get a ranked opportunity list you can act on.

### Step 2: Write an AI acceptable use policy

A usage policy tells employees which tools are approved, what data they can and cannot enter, and when a human must review AI output before it goes out. Keep it to a page or two so people actually read it. Name an owner who approves new tools.

**How I help:** I write usage policies and guardrails, including for regulated industries such as healthcare, where patient data stays out of public tools and expert review comes before anything publishes.

### Step 3: Pick one pilot workflow

Choose a workflow that repeats often, follows clear steps, and is low risk if the AI gets something wrong. Monthly reporting, first drafts of routine documents, and research summaries are common starting points. Measure how long the work takes before the pilot, so you can compare after.

**How I help:** I set up one workflow end to end, with prompts, review steps, and a simple way to measure results.

### Step 4: Choose and connect the right tools

Most teams should start with well-configured off-the-shelf tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, plus automation that connects them to the systems you already use. Build custom only where the workflow is specific to your business or your data cannot leave your systems.

**How I help:** I handle tool and model selection and write build-or-buy plans. When custom software is the right answer, I scope and build it through [AI engineering](/services/ai-engineering/).

### Step 5: Train your team and manage the change

Training is the step companies skip most easily. Only [39% of AI users had received training from their employer](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2024/05/08/microsoft-and-linkedin-release-the-2024-work-trend-index-on-the-state-of-ai-at-work/) in Microsoft's 2024 survey. Train people on the exact workflow they will run. Document each step so a new hire can run it.

**How I help:** I run team training on prompting and AI-assisted research, and I document processes so non-technical staff can run them reliably.

### Step 6: Measure results, then scale to every department

Once the pilot shows measurable time saved or quality gained, repeat the pattern in the next department. Track a few numbers per workflow: hours saved, error rate, and cost per task. Review them every quarter and retire what does not pay off.

**How I help:** I build the rollout plan and backlog, and I automate the reporting so results show up without anyone rebuilding a spreadsheet.

## How long does AI implementation take?

A first AI workflow can be running in weeks. In my projects, a focused prototype that proves one workflow usually takes two to four weeks. A production tool with sign-in, monitoring, and integrations takes longer. A company-wide rollout happens in phases, one department at a time, and its pace depends on how many workflows you cover and how much custom building they need.

Most of the time goes into agreeing on what a good output looks like, who reviews it, and how you will measure it. Settling those questions early is what keeps a pilot from stalling.

## Should you build custom AI tools or buy them?

Buy first, and build only where off-the-shelf tools cannot do the job. Custom tools make sense when a workflow is specific to your business, when your data cannot leave your systems, or when you repeat a process often enough that packaging it as software pays off.

Building has become cheaper. McKinsey's 2026 survey found that [nearly a third of organizations decided against buying at least one software product because they could build it in-house with AI coding tools](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai). Still, a custom tool you did not need is one of the most expensive AI mistakes a business can make.

## Do you need an AI implementation consultant?

You need one if you want results faster than your team can learn by trial and error, or if your industry has rules that make mistakes costly. Research supports outside help: MIT NANDA's study of enterprise AI projects found that [buying from specialized vendors and building with partners succeeded about 67% of the time, while internal builds succeeded far less often](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/).

|  | Do it yourself | Work with an AI implementation consultant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed to first result | Depends on who has spare time | Planned pilot with a set timeline |
| Choosing use cases | Often whichever ideas come up first | Ranked by time saved, risk, and effort |
| Policy and risk | Often written after a problem | Written before rollout |
| Training | Self-taught, uneven across the team | Built around your actual workflows |
| Custom tools | Hard to scope without experience | Scoped, built, or ruled out early |
| Cost | Staff time and trial and error | Consulting fees, with a defined scope |

Doing it yourself can work for a small team with a curious, technical owner and low-risk workflows. Most businesses moving from zero AI to AI in every workflow gain more from a short, structured engagement that sets the foundation their team then runs.

AI workflow automation is one part of AI integration services. Automation handles a specific sequence of steps. Integration services also cover the assessment, policy, tool selection, training, and measurement that make automation work across a business.

### What should an AI acceptable use policy include?

A usage policy should list approved tools, define what data can and cannot go into them, state when human review is required, name who approves new tools, and explain how employees report problems. One or two pages is enough for most businesses.
